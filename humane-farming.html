Beyond the Resume

On Animals & Humane Farming

A love for animals I've had since before I can remember, and the research it's led me to. This is a personal position, not an argument that everyone should go vegan, just an honest look at the alternatives.

I want to share my story that led me into this, starting with a love for animals since birth, having fostered them my whole life. Dogs were being fostered in my family even before I was born in 2008, and we still foster today. I plan to continue.

On a funny note, I was vegetarian starting at 8, solely because my parents made me eat meatloaf and I didn't like the taste. That was reason enough for me to cut out all meat. Over time I learned the benefits, not just for me but for the animals too. Running became more difficult, so I started eating chicken and eventually fish; after 3 years I was no longer vegetarian. It is doable, though. I just needed to track my macros to make sure I was getting enough protein, instead of eyeballing a handful of almonds.

In the past 2 years, I've done a lot of research on U.S. industrial agriculture, for the animals' sake. Full veganism felt nearly impossible on top of saving money and running, but I no longer felt comfortable consuming dairy after understanding the truth of the dairy industry. I hated denying the truth just because it's easier or more comfortable.

There is so much in life made to make things easier, but at a certain point it does the opposite; it makes the slightest discomfort feel terrible, so people avoid it entirely. But humans thrive off a little discomfort. It's what causes you to adapt.

When the topic of how meat gets to the table comes up, people squirm and look away so they can feel better. I've been yelled at before, simply because I couldn't even get heard.

My goal isn't to make the whole world vegan; that's a personal decision. It's to make people aware of the alternatives, instead of defaulting to mass production. Family farms also benefit the farmers, unlike mass production, where cows are confined.

What the research shows

Piglet crushing, when a sow accidentally lies on her newborns, is one of the leading causes of piglet death in commercial pig farming, accounting for roughly a third to more than half of preweaning mortality across various studies. Undercover footage from the documentary Dominion (2018, dir. Chris Delforce), filmed inside Australian factory farms and slaughterhouses, showed overcrowded farrowing pens where crushed piglets were left in place rather than removed. Notably, several studies also find that individual farrowing crates are associated with fewer crushing deaths than loose group housing, a genuine tension in animal-welfare research, not a simple case of more space always meaning fewer deaths.

Cows, mothers, and calves

Ideally, we would only choose dairy alternatives, because either way the cows have their babies taken away from them so we can have the milk, which is sad.

But I know it's not an ideal world, and I can't force everyone to give up dairy. Artificial insemination is standard practice on dairy farms because it's safer than keeping a bull. Bulls caused 48 percent of cattle-related fatalities despite making up only 2 percent of cattle, and insemination reduces the risk to both people and cows.4 That's why it's important that it's done by a professional, to make sure it isn't hurting the cow.

I don't eat dairy because I find artificial insemination disturbing, and even more disturbing is that the mother and her calf are separated. This page is about alternative resources, though, and better options are a step in the right direction. I can't force anyone, but I can share them.

The male calves get slaughtered, and so do the mothers once their milk production is done. I hate that they're used up and thrown out like trash, when they have feelings too and are a life, just like dogs. If you wouldn't do it to your dog, don't do it to another animal just because we've normalized it.

What the research shows

Dairy cows are removed from herds long before old age. In the Northeast, the average cull rate was 31.4 percent per year, and the leading reasons were poor production and infertility.5 Dairy cattle can live 15 to 20 years naturally, but dairy cows are usually sent to slaughter at around four to six years old, once their milk production drops.6

I've cut out dairy because I don't believe the taste is worth having a mother and child torn apart, especially when we have so many alternatives to dairy.

What the research shows

We have evidence that cows form genuine maternal bonds, recognize their own calves, and that the timing and duration of separation affects their behavior and the strength of that bond.1, 2, 3 If we know an animal has a meaningful maternal relationship and we can avoid deliberately disrupting that relationship for a human food preference, there is an ethical reason to question whether separating dairy cows from their calves is justified.

Using cows for their milk, and slaughtering them, is hard to justify, especially when Impossible meat and other alternatives don't taste that bad.

If people are going to eat these animals, at least be aware of what that means. Even if you're not vegan, switching to almond milk instead of regular milk spares a mother cow from having her calf taken from her.

I don't say any of this to scare or judge anyone. I'm not shunning people who eat meat; as a student headed into nutrition, I understand the nutritional benefits well. I'm just saying: know your options.

So I took the time to research brands that are humane toward animals. What I can't stand is when people reject the truth only because it's the norm they've lived in for years.

In an ideal world, I could convince everyone that we don't need to eat animals, and that the alternatives taste just as good. But there are steps until then, so for starters, here are my resources.

What I Look For

  • Real pasture access, animals can move, graze, and behave naturally, not just "cage-free" on paper.
  • A natural diet, grass and forage instead of purely grain-based confinement feeding.
  • No forced/artificial insemination, non-negotiable for me, even though it's genuinely rare across the industry, even on small farms.
  • The most humane end possible, the animal gets a life before it gets a death.
  • Family-farm sourced, ideally a farm with a name and a face behind it, not a supply chain.

Certifications, Explained

Certified Humane

Run by Humane Farm Animal Care. Covers a range from enriched indoor housing up through full pasture-raised, the "Free Range" and "Pasture Raised" tiers are the ones that match what I look for.

Animal Welfare Approved

Run by A Greener World. The most stringent of the major labels, pasture-based only, no indoor confinement tier to fall back on.

Global Animal Partnership (GAP)

A tiered system, Step 1 through 5+. Step 1 just means no cages indoors; Step 5+ means the animal spent its whole life on one farm with real outdoor access. The step number matters as much as the label itself.

Brands I've Found

My working list, mostly family farms directly, or family-farm brands that sell through markets like Kimberton Whole Foods and Wegmans. This grows as I find more.

Chicken

Bell & Evans, family-owned in Pennsylvania since 1894, raised without cages or antibiotics, with real outdoor access.

Eggs

Wegmans Pasture-Raised Grade A Large Brown Eggs, true pasture access, not just cage-free on the label.

Goat Cheese

Blue Ledge Farm, Capriole, Dove Song Dairy

Milk & Yogurt

Farmer Joe's Farm, one of the only brands I found that states outright: no artificial insemination, natural breeding only. Raw milk and drinkable yogurt from cows and goats.

Pork

Tussock Sedge Farm, heritage-breed pigs that breed, farrow, and live entirely on pasture. Worth noting: I couldn't find a pork brand that states "no artificial insemination" as explicitly as Farmer Joe's does for dairy, pasture breeding strongly implies it, but I'd ask directly before assuming.

Farm practices and ownership can change. I'd rather you double-check a farm's current standards yourself before trusting a label, mine included.

Cows & Calves: The Research

  • 1. Nevard, R.P. et al. (2023). Maternal Behavior in Beef Cattle: The Physiology, Assessment and Future Directions, A Review. Veterinary Sciences, 10(1), 10. Reviews how cows bond with their calves right after birth, can tell their own calf from others after only 24 hours together, and show much less maternal interest when the calf is removed immediately.
  • 2. Wenker, M.L. et al. (2020). Effect of cow-calf contact on cow motivation to reunite with their calf. Scientific Reports, 10, 14233. Cows pushed weighted gates to reach their calves, and cows that had been allowed to suckle pushed the hardest.
  • 3. Meagher, R.K. et al. (2019). A systematic review of the effects of prolonged cow-calf contact on behavior, welfare, and productivity. Journal of Dairy Science, 102(7), 5765-5783. Most studies of extended contact found benefits for social behavior.
  • 4. University of Minnesota Extension. Handling bulls safely. Bulls caused 48 percent of cattle-related fatalities while making up 2 percent of cattle, and artificial insemination reduces the risk to people and cows.
  • 5. Penn State Extension. Cull Rates: How is Your Farm Doing? Uses USDA/NAHMS data on why dairy cows are removed from herds.
  • 6. Compassion in World Farming. The life of dairy cows (an animal-welfare charity, so read it as advocacy). On natural lifespan versus age at slaughter, and what happens to male calves.
